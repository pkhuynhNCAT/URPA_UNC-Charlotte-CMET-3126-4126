Pathfinder · Station 07
A friendly guide to learning construction scheduling
Version 4.1 · Readability Edition · September 15, 2026
For UNC Charlotte — CMET 3126/4126 — Project Planning, Scheduling, and Control
You are helping a crew plan a fire station. Some jobs can happen together. Other jobs must wait. Your job is to work out when the station can finish and which delays really matter.
You learn by trying a small decision, watching what happens, and explaining why. Pip, the little guide in the hard hat, tells you what to do next. You do not need to know scheduling formulas before you begin.
The only file you need to play is `Pathfinder_Station07.html`. This README explains how to use it. No installation, account, internet connection, or AI subscription is needed after downloading the file.

2. What will I learn for class?
The game practices the Critical Path Method, usually called CPM. CPM uses the order and duration of jobs to calculate a project schedule.
By working through the missions, you practice answering these questions:
Practical question	Class idea
What must finish before this crew can start?	Activities, dependencies, and network arrows
What is the earliest this job can start or finish?	Forward pass, early start, and early finish
How late can this job start or finish while protecting the project finish?	Backward pass, late start, and late finish
Can this job be delayed without making the project late?	Total float
Can this job be delayed without making the next crew start later?	Free float
Which complete route controls the project finish?	Critical path
If one duration changes, does the same route still control?	Recalculating a schedule and recognizing a changed critical path
These activities support the course work on preparing and analyzing construction schedules and understanding schedule risk. The game is a practice companion to the lectures and problem sets. It covers a focused part of the course rather than every scheduling topic.
3. How do I learn by playing?
Use this short routine throughout the app:
Read the large question and Pip’s instruction. Work on that one task.
Try the action. You might tap a job, enter a number, choose an answer, or press Play.
Look at the result. Read the explanation as well as watching the animation.
Say why it happened. A short sentence is enough: “This crew waits because the other job is still working.”
Continue when it makes sense. Press the next-step button. You decide when to move on.
A prediction is a guess you can test. It is not graded for being right or wrong. A surprising result is useful because it gives you something to explain.
For calculation questions, an incorrect answer gives you another chance. There are no countdowns, lost lives, leaderboards, or penalties for asking for help.
You do not need to explore every menu. Follow the main journey first. The Field guide and extra demonstrations are there when you want them.
If you feel stuck
Press Help at the top. The first message explains your current task and the next action. Expand the extra help only if you need it:
How do I use the simulation? explains Play, stepping through days, handoffs, and the slider.
What do the scheduling words mean? explains the terms in plain language.
How do I save, leave, or start over? explains backups and replaying.
On a calculation screen, I need a hint points toward the governing rule. Ask again to see the rule in symbols. After two unsuccessful attempts on that calculation, Show the worked answer becomes available. The answer stays visible until you choose to continue.
Pip uses explanations built into the file. It is an offline guide, so it cannot answer arbitrary chat questions or automatically judge the quality of your written reasoning.

5. Follow the three core missions
Mission 1 — Find the route
You see a six-job site plan, called Network W.
Start by tapping W1.
Follow the arrows by choosing the highlighted next job. At a split, choose the branch you think will take longer.
Continue to W6. You have now traced one complete route.
Add the days on your selected route. If you want to change your route, use Undo last tap.
Predict how long the whole project will take, then press Save my guess.
Follow the six forward-pass examples. Reveal one calculation, read it, then move on.
Follow the six backward-pass examples in the same way. The job arrows still point right. A separate blue arrow shows that you are calculating backward.
Compare your prediction with both complete routes, then continue.
The main lesson: look at the time along a complete route. Choosing the longest individual job does not necessarily identify the critical path.
The first round is a guided worked example. You will enter the class calculations yourself in Mission 2.
Mission 2 — Build the schedule
You now work on the ten-job fire-station plan, called Network A.
The game asks for 16 selected answers, one at a time. You do not have to fill every cell of a large table. The selected questions focus on places where a scheduling decision matters.
The work has three parts:
Part	Your task	The rule to remember
Early dates	Find the earliest starts and finishes.	Wait for every incoming job, then add this job’s duration.
Latest dates	Work backward from the project finish.	Finish in time for every immediate next job, then subtract this job’s duration.
Room to move	Find total float and free float.	Protecting the project finish and protecting the next crew’s start are different promises.
Before each part, Pip gives you a tiny practice question with different numbers. Then you return to the class plan.
For each class calculation:
Read which job and which number you need.
Look at the relevant values in the small picture.
Enter one number.
Press Check my answer.
Read the explanation, then press Next small step. The final calculation uses See what your schedule means.

The short written explanation
After the calculations, you explain what happens when job E takes one extra day.
Write one or two sentences answering both questions:
Does the next crew, F, start later?
Does the whole project finish later, and why?
Press Save and see what happens. Compare your words with the worked explanation and try the two crew-handoff views. Then choose I compared my reasoning · Continue.
Your writing is saved. Pip does not automatically grade it. If the reflection is used for assessment, your instructor should review the reasoning.
Mission 3 — Try a change
Four crew reports change the plan. Each report follows the same four screens:
Screen	What you do
1. Guess	Read the changed duration and predict the finish of the whole project. Save your guess.
2. Watch	Play the changed schedule. Follow all ten crews from A to J and read the handoff captions.
3. Find the route	Compare the complete-route bars. Select the longest route. Select all tied longest routes if there is a tie.
4. Explain	Compare your prediction with what happened and read why the finish changed or stayed the same.
Each report starts again from the original plan. You do not carry a delay from one report into the next.
The reports help you discover when a delay fits inside available float, when it runs beyond that float, and when another route takes control.
In the last report, try Recover 1 day, Recover 2 days, and Recover 3 days. Look for the check mark on each amount. Notice whether saving more time on steel keeps saving time for the whole project. Explore all three amounts to finish the mission.
If your selected route is incorrect, compare the complete day totals and try again. After two unsuccessful route checks, Walk me through the route is available.

10. A small scheduling dictionary
Term	Plain meaning
Activity	One job in the plan. Letters such as A, B, and C identify the jobs.
Duration	How many working days a job takes.
Dependency	A rule saying one job must finish before another can start.
Predecessor	A job immediately before the job you are considering.
Successor	A job immediately after the job you are considering.
Network	A picture of jobs and the arrows connecting them.
Path or route	A continuous chain of jobs following arrows from the beginning to the end.
Join or merge	A point where a job waits for more than one incoming job.
Branch or split	A point where one job leads to more than one next job.
Forward pass	Working from the beginning to find the earliest start and finish of each job.
Backward pass	Working back from the calculated project finish to find the latest allowed starts and finishes.
Early start / early finish (ES / EF)	The earliest a job can start or finish while respecting the arrows.
Late start / late finish (LS / LF)	The latest a job can start or finish while preserving the calculated project finish.
Total float (TF)	How far a job can be delayed without delaying the project finish, with the rest of the plan unchanged.
Free float (FF)	How far a job can be delayed without delaying an immediate successor’s early start.
Critical path	A complete route whose duration equals the project duration. More than one route can tie.
Float is shared along a route. You cannot assume that several jobs can each use all of their displayed float independently without recalculating the plan.
The formulas, when you are ready
Find this	Use this rule
Early start	Largest early finish among the immediate predecessors. A starting job begins at 0.
Early finish	Early start + duration
Project duration	Largest early finish among the terminal jobs
Late finish	Smallest late start among the immediate successors. A terminal job uses the calculated project finish.
Late start	Late finish − duration
Total float	Late start − early start, or late finish − early finish
Free float	Smallest successor early start − this job’s early finish
Terminal-job free float	Project duration − that terminal job’s early finish
A three-day job starting at time 0 finishes at time 3: 0 + 3 = 3. Time 0 is the beginning of the first workday. These are working-day boundaries, not calendar dates.
11. Notes for instructors
Use in class
Distribute the HTML as a downloadable file alongside this guide. Explain the first click, then let students work at their own pace. For a beginner, the useful question is often “What is this crew waiting for?” before asking for a formula.
One possible teaching sequence is to use the opening and route mission first, assign the schedule calculations next, and discuss the four changed plans after students have practiced float. This is a suggested pacing approach, not an estimated completion time.
The source brief suggested about 25–30 minutes for the original core activity. This edition includes additional guided practice. Allow flexible time or divide it across sessions. Its completion time has not been measured with students.
To check understanding, ask a student to explain a handoff, distinguish total from free float, or use a rule with a new set of numbers. A completed notebook is participation evidence. Written reasoning needs human review if used for assessment.
The app has no instructor dashboard or automatic gradebook connection. The notebook and screenshot are the available submission records. Because the HTML and progress files are editable, they are not secure examination certificates.
Scope and assumptions
The module follows the supplied concept brief:
Activity-on-node networks with finish-to-start relationships and zero lag.
Positive, whole working-day durations.
A deterministic schedule: the same durations and arrows produce the same answer.
Forward pass, backward pass, total float, free float, and all tied critical paths.
Independent duration changes in the four required reports. Simultaneous changes only in the optional recovery challenge.
Calendar dates, weekends, resource leveling, cost loading, other relationship types, imposed deadlines, negative float, activity-on-arrow exercises, and commercial scheduling software are outside this module.
The opening examples and optional new-number questions are teaching additions. The source networks W and A retain their original activities, dependencies, and durations.

12. Accessibility, privacy, and file maintenance
Use Tab to move through controls, Enter to activate a button or submit a number, Space for buttons and checkboxes, and Escape to close a dialog. Focus a time slider and use the arrow keys to move it. The Text size control enlarges the app to 125%, 150%, or 200%. Browser zoom is also available.
The app includes visible focus indicators, labeled inputs, text equivalents for networks, plain-language feedback, and reduced-motion controls. Critical activities and routes are marked with words or symbols as well as color. Sound is optional and off by default. No lesson depends on hearing it.
The app stores progress locally when available. It has no analytics, external runtime assets, automatic uploads, or cloud synchronization. Opening an optional source link uses the internet. The information in a downloaded notebook or backup is shared only when someone chooses to share that file.
Editing the file
All required code, styles, illustrations, calculations, and text are inside the HTML. No build command is needed to use or edit the deliverable. Open it in a text editor, save your changes, and reopen or refresh it in a browser.

14. The final design and readability update
Version 4.1 — Readability Edition keeps the faculty corrections and changes how the app looks and reads.
What changed
Larger text throughout the working screens. Small 10–14-pixel labels have been replaced with a consistent type scale. Instructions are usually 19 pixels, secondary text is at least 16 pixels, and dates in the full network are 17 pixels at standard size.
A saved text-size control. Students can choose 100%, 125%, 150%, or 200%. Text, controls, and diagram dimensions adapt together. Earlier progress files still work.
A clearer first action. “Send the first crew” now appears immediately after the opening explanation.
A construction field-guide appearance. The design uses simpler borders, a quieter background, more distinct headings, and fewer decorative panels. Pip is a human guide in a hard hat.
More useful graphics. The station drawing keeps its crane, crew movements, and construction stages. Decorative sparkles, floating clouds, background circles, and tiny words inside the artwork have been removed. The important activity names remain in readable text outside the illustration.
Readable phone timelines. Each job’s name, status, dates, and progress track have enough room. On narrow screens, the track moves below the label instead of squeezing the text into a small column.
An alternative to shrinking the network. “Read as text” shows jobs and available dates in a list. It replaces the old “Fit whole network” button, which could make the labels too small. Sideways scrolling still lets you inspect every dependency in the full drawing.
Stronger contrast. Unrevealed table cells, placeholder text, and secondary labels are easier to distinguish from their backgrounds.
